# A weekend of chess: 4 Nations Chess League 

It’s October. For some, school is starting. For others, the summer of internships or vacationing is over, and it’s time to join a grad scheme or update their CV. For me, the chess season is kicking off! When I was junior, summer often meant chess. Since during the school year going to a tournament entailed missing school, the opportunities were often more limited. So the summer break was the perfect moment to go away for a week and spend 3 to 6 hours in some hopefully air-conditioned-but-most-likely-not room playing chess. I started playing chess at 7 years old, and there was never a summer I didn’t play at least one tournament over the summer until I turned 18. Nowadays, I have the opposite schedule. The London Chess League and the UK’s Four Nations Chess League span over the months of October through May to form what in my life represents the “chess season”. While I would love to go to a chess tournament in the summer, preferably somewhere in Spain where I can get to the beach and back before the game starts, I unfortunately did not make that happen in 2026. I blame London’s weather. We’re often so deprived of sun during the rest of the year, that you just can’t forgive yourself for staying too much inside (such as 4 hours per classical game) during the sunny season. <br>
The Four Nations Chess League is a teams competition, and it consists of 5 divisions. There is a demotion and promotion system between divisions, with Division 1 being the most competitive. You can think of its format like the one of the Olympiad, but instead of countries, you have clubs. Teams position their eight players according to some rating gap rules, and the line-ups become public 2 hours before the match start. The players alternate colors - so board 1 of one team plays white, then their board 2 plays black, and so on. Each player’s result is counted towards the team’s total, and the team who scores the most points at the end of all games wins the match. <br>
One quirky thing about the 4NCL is the way it’s organized. Most classical chess tournaments take up at five to nine days all at once. Instead, the 4NCL plays eleven rounds in six months. Two rounds per weekend, one weekend once everyfew weeks. One game on Saturday, one on Sunday, and see you next time! At first, I was skeptical about this format. In terms of effort, you end up traveling back and forth much more than in a traditional system. I also worried this would make it hard to get into rhythm. But over the years (this is my fourth year playing the 4NCL!), I came to appreciate it. Firstly, it is much more inclusive. Not everyone has the time to just book a week away from work for chess, and pay a hotel for a whole week. People can come on the weekend, and can choose to not play the whole thing. Since teams usually have more players in the squad than the eight that they need for a day's match, they can rotate people to give them flexibility about how many weekends they want to attend. Secondly, the worry I had about getting into rhythm can also be a plus: if you have a rocky start or a frustrating loss, you can go home, relax, train some more, and avoid ruining the rest of your games because of bad form. Thirdly, this way of organizing shows awareness of the realities of being a non-professional strong chess player. Even as a titled player, you cannot reliably make a living out of playing alone. Given this limited funding  in competitive chess in general, it would be unfair to force people to drop their other commitments, and equally unfair to force them to miss on one of the UK’s most competitive tournaments. <br>
Another quirky thing about the 4NCL, and about teams competitions in general, is that the way you go from one opponent to the next feels completely different than in an open tournament. In an open, if you win, you climb to the top and you face stronger and stronger opponents. If you start losing, you usually start facing weaker and weaker opponents as well. But at 4NCL, all teams face each other eventually. That basically makes it so that you might play terribly on a Saturday, and then find out you’re facing a GM on Sunday. (This has happened to me, went home with nil out of two, yikes.) What also happened to me last year, in Division one, is despite struggling through the games and ending with 2.5/11 (which was decent, everything considered), all my opponents had a rating of over 2000, and only two of them were under 2100. <br>
This weekend, I played in Division Two. And although it's not as intense as Divion One, I am actually welcoming the opportunity to win more. Both my opponents this weekend were around 2000 FIDE, and since my current rating is 2010, that makes it very achievable for me to win and grow my rating. But this also puts more pressure on me. (Pressure from whom? From myself, of course. If you watch me on Twitch, you know I have no chill when it comes to chess). Last year, when most of my games were against titled players, losing sucked less, because it was the expected result. And even though I had a couple of devastating throwaways, I couldn’t be too hard on myself. This year, winning is much more the expected result if I want to approach the 2100 milestone. 
### Game one 
I started this game in a really bad mood. I had planned to make it to the venue two hours before the game was supposed to start. But multiple trains from London towards the North-West were cancelled in the last moment. So I made it 45 minutes late to the game, with no time to catch my breath or check into my hotel room, after a stressful re-scheduling of the itinerary. The time control at 4NCL is 90 minutes plus 30 seconds increment, with 30 minutes extra time after move 40. So starting the game with 50 minutes left didn’t mean I couldn’t still win, but it can prove to be a huge disadvantage if the game is complex from early on. During the game, I knew I needed to try to close the gap on the clock between me and my opponent, but I just couldn’t. Not having played classical in five months, feeling tired and annoyed, I just couldn’t feel confident enough to talk myself into moving faster. <br>
The first critical moment of the game came here. After trades on d5, I thought the position is around equal, and I was looking for some quick stable moves I can play fast. But my opponent surprised me by recapturing with the bishop, which immediately felt off. After a quick Na4 and his response Nd7, I felt I had taken over the game. But I had 6 minutes left, and 16 moves to play until the extra time. I burn through the remainder of my time calculating B takes a6, and by the time I put it on the board, I had 2 minutes left. I couldn’t sacrifice my bishop in 10 seconds, but to be honest, I played it out of the sunken cost fallacy as well. I didn’t have enough time to convince myself it was working (and it wasn’t), but I had already burnt through the rest of my time calculating it, so might as well go for it. My opponent is trying to play fast, even though he has time on the clock. This is a common strategy to not allow the player in time trouble to think on your time, force them into making a mistake, and then actually spend time finding the punishment to their error. So he doesn’t find the right way to punish my overly enthusiastic bishop sacrifice and .. I’m winning. 
I know I’m winning, but I know it’s slippery because of the time control. And even though I’m better, I’m so much more stressed than before. Because now I have something to ruin, a position to then beat myself up for ruining. Which, spoiler alert, is exactly what happened. 
I miss an opportunity to simplify thinking I still have time later on. I’m trying to keep up the pressure, but the truth is, instead of looking for wins, my mind is bracing for jump-scare comebacks from my opponent to spawn on the board at any moment. 
I make my first blunder. It’s a crazy blunder - for some reason, I just forgot that he can take twice on c3. I am trying to keep a straight face while he’s considering this, half-praying that he would be scared the bishop move is actually a genius sacrifice. He goes for it, of course, but the position is still better for me. I know I still have some chess to at least draw, so I try to push the screams of “YOU F*CKED IT!!!” far from my mind. But I’m destabilized. So when Qb4 comes, I start panicking. The seconds are ticking down the clock, and I just don’t have time nor clarity to see if the two knight checks he’s threatening are actually scary or just look scary. Nine, opt, seven .. I grab my king, and then it’s over. 
I went into my room in a rage. But the rage doesn’t last long. And I’ve lost enough winning positions to know that behind any fury, there’s heartbreak. Ultimately, I felt like my opponent didn’t defeat me - I defeated myself. But that I wouldn’t be fair to my opponent, and regardless of how competitive and fierce I feel sometimes, he saw his chance and he took it. 
Luckily, I ended up feeling mostly ok by the end of the evening. I decided that the fact that I got a winning position was a good sign, and if that happens again tomorrow, I won’t miss. I also decided to completely blame it on the trains, of course. But, don’t you worry, I did wait until the next day to file my complaint with the train operator. 
Game two 
The pairings are out 90 minutes before each game, which gives players a little bit more than an hour to prepare their openings. (Looking up your opponent’s name and games on the internet and then revisit your repertoire is very very common in chess tournaments. It sometimes makes for a funny vibe in hotels’ lobbies - everyone with their phones or laptops moving chess pieces back and forth.) I saw that my opponent likes playing the classical line of The French Defense, and then go for the aggressive setup with long castle. I was both thrilled and apprehensive upon learning this. Thrilled because in a position with opposite-side castling, anything can happen, giving me great winning (and losing) chances. And I wanted to make sure I go for the win - after the previous loss, I was meant to lose 11.6 rating points, which would put my new rating at the infuriating value of 1999. And a draw wouldn’t quite cut it, giving me just 1.2 points, meaning I’d be at FIDE’s mercy to stay at 2000 or drop back to the ugly 19-prefix rating. But I was also apprehensive because I hadn’t had great results against this system in the past. I did my best to prepare, and, luckily enough, my opponent did play into one of the lines I had glanced at in the morning. 
Placing the Knight on c4 was the end of my preparation, but I felt good knowing that I had looked at some typical ideas earlier in the day. The thing with this kind of positions is that they are very scary and they can become cut-throat very suddenly. White’s plan usually involves trying to push f5, and if allowed f6. If black tries to stop this with g6, this weakens the dark squares, and white can choose between doubling up on the h file, planting a knight onto f5, or pushing h4-h5. The way my opponent played it with g5 first is also very valid. Black also has some typical ideas in its arsenal, but the delicacy of the position is choosing when to do what. Black’s main sacrifice idea is Knight takes b2, destabilizing the knight on c3. Black also wants to play b4 and get the Queen through a5, or in some positions b4-a5-Ba5 to exchange white’s powerful bishop on d3. 
A few moves and trades later, I decide to bring my knight back to e7. This move was multi-purpose for me. On one hand, I send that knight towards the kingside for defensive purposes. For example, if White threatens mate with Qh5, I can now choose between playing g6 or Ng6. The knight also holds the f5 and d5 squares, since white is always on the look-out for some f5 & N takes d5 ideas. On the other hand, I open up my rooks way towards the white king.
